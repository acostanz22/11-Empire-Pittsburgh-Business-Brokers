Questions owners in this market ask

Is Pittsburgh still only a mill-town market?

No. Healthcare, universities, robotics, and neighborhood services are a large share of what we see. Manufacturing still matters. The offering has to match the file you have.

Will out-of-town buyers understand the hills?

Not at first. We put access, parking, and crew commute in the memorandum so the first visit is not a shock.

How do you value a clinic tied to a hospital system?

On production, payer mix, and what is personal goodwill. A vendor logo is not a transferable enterprise by itself.

Can I sell without telling the floor?

Through marketing, yes. Through closing, people will know. We plan the message so you do not lose the technicians who make the P&L true.

For owners

10 things you need to know before selling your business

Owners preparing to sell a business in Pittsburgh usually underestimate the same issues. These are the ones that change price, timing, and whether a deal survives diligence.

Employees, customers, and competitors should not learn you are selling from a public listing or a careless conversation. A business broker uses confidentiality agreements, screened inquiries, and controlled disclosure so value is not damaged before you are ready to talk.
Owners often remember the year the shop was slammed or the contract that almost closed. Buyers, lenders, and SBA underwriters start with tax returns, interim statements, and add-backs that can be proven. Clean books shorten diligence.
If the company only works because you take every call, hold every relationship, and make every decision, a buyer sees risk. Document processes, introduce key staff, and show that revenue can transfer.
Owner salary, one-time legal fees, personal auto, and discretionary perks can be normalized — but only with support. Inflated add-backs are one of the fastest ways to lose a serious buyer.
Inventory, receivables, and payables move with the business. Agreeing early on a target working-capital level prevents last-week fights over cash left in the company.
The headline price is not what you keep. Structure, allocation, and taxes can swing six figures. Coordinate your CPA and attorney with the broker before you accept a letter of intent.
SBA 7(a) and conventional loans still fund a large share of Main Street and lower-middle-market deals. That means the business must be financeable: verifiable earnings, reasonable leverage, and a story a lender can defend.
If you own the building, you may sell it with the company, retain it and lease it back, or keep it as an investment. Those choices affect price, financing, and your income after closing.
Buyers pay for a going concern. Retention of managers, technicians, and producers should be planned, not improvised after the announcement.
The owners who net more are usually the ones who spent a season tightening reporting, customer concentration, and leases — then went to market on purpose, not in a rush.
